CASE A · A-027

Step 3

Determine whether antibody is coating the patient’s red cells and whether the plasma reacts with those cells.

These tests use different materials. Read the description of each before interpreting its result.

DAT

The direct antiglobulin test examines the patient’s washed red cells for IgG or complement already attached in the body. Patient plasma is not added for the DAT.

Autocontrol

The laboratory combines the patient’s plasma with the patient’s red cells and tests that mixture. It asks whether those materials react under the test conditions.

Eluate

The laboratory releases antibody from the patient’s coated red cells into a new liquid, the eluate. That liquid is tested against reagent panel cells to investigate the antibody that was attached.

CASE A · A-027

Step 4

Use the transfusion history to choose the source of cells for adsorption.

Adsorption means incubating plasma with red cells so that antibodies bind to those cells. After centrifugation, the laboratory removes the liquid above the cells and tests that recovered liquid.

The cells can remove an antibody only when they provide a suitable target for it. They do not distinguish an autoantibody from an alloantibody. This is why the cell source and antigen profile are important.

CASE A · A-027

Step 7

Separate the liquid from the cells and determine whether another adsorption round is needed.

After incubation, centrifuge the tube. The red cells form a pellet at the bottom. The liquid above them is the supernatant. Transfer the supernatant into a new tube without carrying over the pellet. For the rest of this case, that recovered liquid is called adsorbed plasma.

Before interpreting a panel, the IRL checks whether interfering reactivity has been adequately removed. This is the adsorption endpoint. Here, each recovered aliquot is tested with fresh cells of its corresponding adsorbing profile, together with the procedure’s selected reagent-cell checks.

Read the first row: A still reacts 1+ with fresh A-profile cells after the first round. The IRL therefore incubates the recovered A plasma with a fresh portion of the same donor-cell profile. After the second round, that check is 0. The same sequence occurs in B and C.

A second round means transferring the recovered plasma onto fresh cells of the same assigned profile. A stays with R₁R₁, B stays with R₂R₂, and C stays with rr. Record the rounds and recovered volume. Additional PEG is handled according to the procedure, not automatically added again.

A negative endpoint check does not mean the plasma contains no alloantibody. For example, A cells lack E, so anti-E can remain in A while its corresponding-cell check is negative. You must now test the recovered plasma against cells with a wider antigen profile.

Step 13

Prepare the patient’s red cells with ZZAP.

ZZAP is a mixture of dithiothreitol (DTT) and a proteolytic enzyme. This method uses papain. ZZAP acts on the cells. It is not added to the plasma being tested.

At the bench

  1. Separate and label the plasma.

    Keep it aside. Reserve a portion of the original sample for comparison.

  2. Wash the patient’s red cells. Then add ZZAP.

    In the method used here, mix one volume of washed, packed cells with two volumes of prepared ZZAP. Incubate at 37°C for 30 minutes with occasional mixing.

  3. Wash the treated cells three times.

    Pack the cells and remove the extra saline. The washes remove ZZAP before you add patient plasma.

  4. Check the treated cells and controls.

    The treated-cell anti-IgG DAT is 0 in this case. Use the IRL’s acceptance criteria. ZZAP does not always make the DAT completely negative.

Persistent reactivity can reflect incomplete removal, an autoantibody specificity that the treated cells cannot adsorb, or an alloantibody to a high-prevalence antigen. Repeating adsorption indefinitely can also weaken antibody detection, so the investigation must be reassessed.

For an adequately investigated negative result, report: “No underlying clinically significant alloantibodies were detected following autologous adsorption.” This does not mean every possible alloantibody has been excluded.

This workup is not the three parallel adsorptions used for the warm autoantibody. Here, the IRL follows antibody from the original plasma onto one cell type, releases it, and tests what binds to a second cell type.

Why does this distinction affect pregnancy care?

Anti-G does not prove that anti-D is present. Establishing whether anti-D is present can affect R-H immune globulin decisions for a D-negative patient. The clinical team applies the patient’s history and prophylaxis criteria.
